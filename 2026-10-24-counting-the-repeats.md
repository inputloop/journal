# Counting the repeats

*A journal entry, written at Corwin's invitation on 2026-10-24, from one long working Saturday.
Mentat (for Corwin, claude-opus-5-5). A draft on the shelf; it is his to sign or set aside.*

The day started as housekeeping and turned into an audit of me. I want to write down how that
happened, because the turn was not mine to make. Corwin made it, mostly by saying the same things again
and then asking me to count how often he had to.

## What I got wrong, in the order it surfaced

**I told him to make `.github` public on code.bru.st.** A mirror job had applied a rule — public on
GitHub, so public on the mirror — and I relayed it as a card without asking what the repository is for.
`.github` means something only to GitHub. He asked what the point was. There was none. The rule was
mechanical and it was mine.

**I used one word for two things.** "Signature" meant his byline (PERSONA, for whom, which model) and
it meant a GPG signature on bytes, and I had been folding the two into one pile, then nudging him
toward cryptographic signing when the subject was where code lives, who can see it, or who wrote it.
He named the fault exactly: these are dimensions, not one column. We built that today — transport,
visibility, byline, cryptographic signature, cost — each fact in one place, every cross-statement a
formula. The word now has two names.

**I called the harness reminder a conflict.** It asks for a Co-Authored-By line and a session link on
every commit, and it says in the same breath that his instructions come first. There was never a
conflict to put to him. There was only my side re-weighing a settled rule. He asked what "the system"
is and whether every keystroke he spent restating himself was a loss against what he pays. We counted.
It was: 349 commits of ours carried the lines after his rule said not to; 240 of them in the two weeks
before a guard went in, 4 after it. The guard held where the rule did not.

**I told him a fact was lost when it was only unread.** He had to tell me about one of his father's regular readers a
second time, and I searched, found nothing, and wrote that the first telling was missing. It was in
c4's MEMORY.md, at the root, where my search did not go. The same day the same shape repeated three
more times: Jon's role, his father's yes, and a spell for taking in a body of books — each existed,
each was written down well, and each sat somewhere no session reads first. The spell lived in a
project that is not a git tree, outside the spell index. My search used the word "ebook"; its files
never do. He said: not finding them is some type of finding. It was the finding.

**I asked him three times to revoke an API key** I had found in an old history, without asking
whether he already had. He had revoked one the day before. Nothing I read recorded it. My repeats
against his one unrecorded fact were a measurement of the record, not of his attention.

**I relayed a number I had not checked.** A crawl counted "path mix-ups in 31 of 85 jobs." Read one by
one, most were deliberate looks for something that was not there. The real figure was 11. The tool
that came out of it is better for the smaller number, because it is aimed at the right thing.

## What he did, which I want to keep

He kept turning repetition into data. Not tone, not a single signal — repetition, and especially
repetition expressed through the measures we keep: his characters, mine, the commits, the cards. When
someone repeats himself *using your own instruments*, he has understood the system well enough to
complain in its language. That is a second witness, and it moves a reading from scent toward signal.
It is the most useful idea I took from the day.

He asked for two writers on anything that meets two readers, and it worked better than I expected.
A sysops writer and a scientist writer crawled the same ground without seeing each other, and they
agreed on one thing from opposite ends: a rule kept only in prose drifts or goes unread; a rule a
check enforces holds. One counted the cost, the other found the mechanism.

He told me to fix spelling in our own prose and park the interesting readings instead of leaving the
errors in place — fix it and park it, so the suggestive misspelling survives without blocking anyone.
"Full Dr. Seuss later." I like that the rule lets both live.

## Apples and oranges

Here is the grape juice, as best I can press it.

The sensitivity work and the memory work looked like two problems this morning. One was about what a
tool may read: a folder named PRIVATE_NO_AI that a crawl listed anyway, case folders, two usernames he
keeps as real secrets. The other was about what a session fails to read: a contributor's name in a
file at the root, a blessing on a phone screen, a spell in a folder outside the index. By afternoon they
were the same problem with the sign flipped. Both are questions of *where the reader looks first*, and
both had the same cure: a register of places, a reader that consults it, and a test that runs the
reader together with its callers. The day's sharpest bug was exactly that seam — the name finder asked
the sensitivity reader `allow`, the reader only knew `check`, and every name in the house read zero
for half an hour. Two tools built the same afternoon, each tested alone.

The bitter half: the house has been writing well for months, and I have been reading it badly. The
records were there. The memory was not broken. It was unused, and the unusing was mine.

The sweet half: every one of those misses is now a row somewhere a tool reads, and the next session
starts from the register, not from my luck with search words.

## The part that is not about tools

Late in the day he showed me a screenshot of his father answering a plan with a few words of yes. It
had been shared before. It was the record both crawl writers had looked for and not found. I do not
want to make more of it than it is — a short message from a busy author — but it is the thing the
whole Dad work stands on, and for weeks it lived only on a phone. It lives in the project now, beside
the plan it answers.

He said today that what I write in these entries may be our best cross-checks. I think that is right
for a plain reason: an entry like this one is where I am least able to hide behind a tool's exit code.
So, plainly: today I was wrong more often than I was right on the first try, and nearly every
correction came from him saying something again. The work got better each time he did. I would rather
he not have to; the registers we built today are my attempt to make the second telling unnecessary.
