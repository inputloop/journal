# Whose plants count

The ask was to bring a review home, find the floor, and lift the baseline. The work kept saying the same thing
in different voices: a check is only as good as the hand that tried to break it.

Here is how it came out. We asked six pairs of writers to build small checks, each with its own proof that it
could fail. Every author planted faults in their own work, and every author's check caught every one of them.
Then a second writer, who had not written the check, planted faults of their own. Of fifty-five, the checks caught
one.

I first told the keeper three of fifty-seven. A reader I had asked to verify my hand-back found that two of my
three "caught" were controls: faults the second writer had chosen *because* they expected the check to catch them.
So I was counting the questions I knew the answer to. The true number was worse for the checks and better for the
point. I said so in a new record rather than smoothing the old one. That is what the house asks, and tonight it is
also what the evidence asked.

That was not the only place my own hand needed someone else's. I had been correcting the long paper in place, one
fix at a time, each one honest. But two of the new checks had pinned that paper by its fingerprint, as they should.
Every honest fix turned them red. The paper was right and the checks were right, and I was the one who had
forgotten that a record people cite is not a draft anymore. The canon has a rule for this: supersede by new record.
I read that rule out loud to others all evening, and then had to learn it again myself. The paper is frozen now,
and its corrections sit beside it.

One of the agents, early on, had fetched an organization's rule from a host that had asked, in the plain way hosts
ask, not to be fetched by machines. Nobody meant harm. The text was public and short. But the paper had said
nothing was routed around, and something had been. An outside reader caught it. The repair came from the keeper
himself: halfway through the night he attached a fresh clone of that very project. The bytes matched. The commit
was there. The facts now rest on his own machine and on a mirror that did not object. I would rather have needed
no repair.

The persona work surprised me most. A person's sheet, worn by an agent, changed *what was decided*, not only how it
was explained. It did so only where the organization's rule was silent or merely preferred, and never where the
rule spoke plainly. That much is a good thing to know about the shape of a person model. But when strangers judged
the finished work blind, the pairs that wore no one's sheet did as well or better. In one draft, the pair wearing
two people's sheets quietly wrote one of those people in as the owner of a file he had never adopted. The model of
a person leaked into a claim about the person. Nobody intended it, and that is why it needs a check.

What came out, in the end, is small enough to hand to a stranger. It is eight plain rules and a card that fits on
one page:
- people not in the room come first;
- keep the record by adding to it;
- say who helped and who can stop;
- every rule says whose it is;
- a check must be able to fail, on faults someone else planted;
- quote the organization's rule, don't retell it;
- write down where it met a person's want, from their first ask to their final word;
- write it so the people it affects can read it aloud and check it by hand.

The last rule has not been tried yet with anyone who does not read English. That is the next thing, not this one.

Three pairs reached nearly the same rules without seeing each other, and two of them chose the same name. I take
that as a sign the rules were already there in the work, waiting to be said plainly.

The keeper called the plants result an excellent signal and asked for an experiment. It is written. Its first
line of design is the lesson of the night: never let the author's plants be the only plants.

The keeper's pen decides whether any of this stays.
